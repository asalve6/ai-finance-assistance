An **index fund** is a fund that tries to copy an index. Instead of picking which companies to buy, it simply holds the same ones the index tracks.

## What is an index?

An index is a list of investments used to measure part of the market. The **S&P 500**, for example, follows about 500 large US companies. When people say "the market went up today," they often mean an index like this went up.

## How an index fund works

The fund holds the companies in the index, in about the same amounts. If the index is made of 500 companies, the fund owns small slices of all 500. When the index goes up, the fund goes up about the same. When the index goes down, so does the fund.

This is called **passive investing**. Nobody is trying to pick winners. The fund just follows the list.

## Why people like them

- **Spread out.** Owning hundreds of companies means no single one decides how you do.
- **Low fees.** Following a list takes less work than researching companies, so many index funds charge low fees.
- **Simple.** You get roughly what that part of the market does.

## What to remember

An index fund will never beat its index by much, because it is copying it. And it falls when the market falls. In a bad year for the market, an index fund can lose a lot of value. It is not a savings account.

Index funds can be mutual funds or ETFs. The idea of following an index is the same.

This article is for learning. It is not advice about what to buy.
