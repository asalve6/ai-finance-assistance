ETF stands for **exchange-traded fund**. It is a way to own small pieces of many investments at once, in a single purchase.

## A basket in one share

Imagine a fruit basket with apples, bananas, and oranges. Instead of buying each fruit on its own, you buy the whole basket. An ETF works like that. One share of an ETF can hold small slices of dozens or even hundreds of companies, or bonds, or other investments.

## Why "exchange-traded"?

ETF shares are bought and sold on a stock exchange, just like shares of a single company. Their price changes during the day as people buy and sell.

## Many ETFs follow an index

Many ETFs try to match an **index**, which is a list of investments used to measure part of the market. For example, an ETF might hold the same companies as an index of large US companies. When those companies' prices move, the ETF moves with them.

## Why people use them

- **Spreading out.** One ETF can hold many companies, so one company's bad day matters less. That is diversification.
- **Simple.** One purchase instead of many.
- **Often low cost.** Funds charge a yearly fee called the **expense ratio**. Many index ETFs have low fees, but fees differ, so people compare them.

## The risks

An ETF is only as steady as what it holds. If the companies inside it fall in price, the ETF falls too. Some ETFs hold riskier things, or use tricky strategies that can swing a lot. An ETF can lose money, just like a single stock.

This article is for learning. It is not advice about what to buy.
