When you buy a share of stock, you own a piece of a company. When you buy a **bond**, you do something different. You lend money.

## A bond is a loan

Governments and companies sometimes need to borrow money. A city might need a new bridge. A company might want to build a factory. They can borrow by selling bonds. Whoever buys the bond is the lender.

## How a bond pays

A bond usually comes with three key promises:

- **Face value:** the amount you get back at the end, like $1,000.
- **Interest:** extra payments along the way, often twice a year. For example, 4% a year on $1,000 is $40 a year.
- **Maturity date:** the day the loan ends and you get your face value back.

## Bonds vs stocks

- A stock is ownership. Its price can rise or fall a lot, and there is no promised payment.
- A bond is a loan with set payments. Bond prices usually move less than stock prices.

That is why people often call bonds "safer." But safer does not mean safe.

## Bonds have risks too

- **The borrower might not pay.** If a company runs into trouble, it may miss payments. Bonds from the US government are considered among the safest, because the government is very likely to pay.
- **Prices change.** If you sell a bond before maturity, you might get less than you paid. When interest rates rise, older bonds with lower rates are worth less.
- **Inflation.** If prices rise faster than your bond's interest, your money buys less.

## Savings bonds

The US Treasury sells **savings bonds** online at TreasuryDirect. Families sometimes give them to kids as gifts. They are a simple way to lend money to the government and earn interest.

This article is for learning. It is not advice about what to buy.

## Try it

Imagine a $1,000 bond that pays 3% interest a year for 5 years. How much interest would it pay each year? How much in total over 5 years, plus the $1,000 back at the end?
