When you keep money in a savings account, the bank pays you a small extra amount for holding it. That extra amount is called interest. You can think of it as a thank-you from the bank.

## Simple interest first

Say you put one hundred dollars in an account that pays five percent each year. After one year, you earn five dollars. If the bank only ever paid interest on your first hundred dollars, you would earn five dollars every year. After two years you would have one hundred ten dollars.

## Where compounding comes in

Most savings accounts work a little differently. In year two, the bank pays interest on your whole balance, including the five dollars you already earned. That is compound interest.

- Start: $100.00
- After year 1: $105.00
- After year 2: $110.25

That extra twenty-five cents does not look like much. It came from interest earning its own interest. Over many years, the gap gets bigger, because each year's growth is built on a larger pile.

## Time is the secret ingredient

The longer money stays in the account, the more time it has to compound. Starting early can matter more than starting big. Someone who saves a small amount for many years can end up with more than someone who saves a larger amount for only a short time.

Rates change, and the numbers here are only examples. Always check the rate that an account really pays.

## Try it

Use a compound interest calculator. The one on Investor.gov is a good place to start. Enter one hundred dollars and a rate of five percent. Compare ten years with twenty years, and notice how much the second ten years adds.
