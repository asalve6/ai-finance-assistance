When the news says "stocks fell today," nobody checked every single company. They looked at a **stock market index**, a kind of scoreboard for a group of companies.

## What an index is

An index follows the prices of a chosen group of companies and turns them into one number. When most of those companies' prices rise, the index rises. When most fall, it falls. It gives a quick picture of how that part of the market is doing.

## Famous US indexes

- **S&P 500:** about 500 large US companies. Bigger companies count more in it. Many people use it as a picture of the whole US stock market.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average:** 30 large, well-known US companies. It is one of the oldest indexes.
- **Nasdaq Composite:** thousands of companies listed on the Nasdaq exchange, including many technology companies.

Other countries have their own indexes too.

## Indexes as a benchmark

People compare investments to an index to see how they did. If your practice portfolio rose 2% in a month while the S&P 500 rose 5%, your portfolio did worse than that benchmark.

## You can't buy an index directly

An index is just a list and a number. But you can buy a fund that copies one, like an index fund or an ETF.

## Indexes move both ways

An index can fall for days, months, or longer. Over long stretches of the past, major US indexes have grown, with many big drops along the way. The past does not promise the future.

This article is for learning. It is not advice about what to buy.

## Try it

With a grown-up, look up the S&P 500 and the Dow Jones today. Did they move the same way? Then check one company from your Penny portfolio. Did it move with the indexes or differently?
