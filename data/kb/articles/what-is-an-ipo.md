Many companies start small and private. The owners and a few early backers own all of it. One day, the company may decide to let the public buy a piece. The first time it does that is called an **IPO**, short for **initial public offering**. People also call it "going public."

## Why do companies go public?

Usually, to raise money. A company might want to build factories, make new products, or hire more people. Selling shares to the public brings in cash to do that. It also lets early owners and workers sell some of their shares if they want to.

## How does it work?

1. **Getting ready.** The company works with banks that help plan the sale. These banks are often called underwriters.
2. **Telling the facts.** In the US, the company files documents with the Securities and Exchange Commission, the SEC. One key document is the **prospectus**. It describes the business, its money, and its risks.
3. **Setting a price.** The company and its banks pick a starting price for the shares and how many to sell.
4. **Trading begins.** The shares are listed on a stock exchange. From then on, anyone with a brokerage account can buy or sell them. The price changes with supply and demand.

## What happens after?

A public company has new duties. It must share regular reports about how it is doing. Its share price is now public, so everyone can see how buyers and sellers feel about it.

## IPO prices can go either way

Some new shares rise after the IPO. Others fall below the starting price. A famous name does not mean the price can only go up. New public companies can be especially hard to judge, because there is less history to look at.

This article is for learning. It is not advice about what to buy.

## Try it

Pick a company you know that is public. Look up the year it first sold shares to the public. Then think about what it might have used the money for.
