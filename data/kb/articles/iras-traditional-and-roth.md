An **IRA**, short for individual retirement arrangement, is a retirement savings account. Unlike a 401(k), you don't need an employer to offer it. People open IRAs on their own at a bank or brokerage.

## An IRA is a container

An IRA is not an investment by itself. It is like a special box. Inside, people can hold savings or investments, such as funds, stocks, or bonds. What's inside can go up or down in value. The box gives tax benefits.

## You need earned income

To put money into an IRA, you need income from work, like a paycheck or money from a job. Allowance and gifts don't count. Each year there is a limit on how much you can add, and it changes over time.

## Traditional IRA

- Money you put in may lower your taxes this year.
- The money can grow without being taxed each year.
- You pay income tax when you take money out in retirement.

## Roth IRA

- You put in money you already paid tax on.
- The money grows without yearly taxes.
- Qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, including the growth.
- People with high incomes may not be allowed to add to a Roth IRA.

## Which is which?

The big difference is when you pay the tax: now (Roth) or later (traditional). People often choose based on whether they expect their tax rate to be higher now or in retirement.

## IRAs for teens

A teen with a real job can have a Roth IRA, opened by a parent as a custodial account. Because a teen's tax rate is often low, paying tax now can make sense, and the money has many years to grow.

## Taking money out early

Taking money out before retirement age can mean taxes and an extra penalty, with some exceptions.

This is for learning, not tax or investment advice.

## Try it

Imagine a teen earns $1,000 from a summer job and puts $200 in a Roth IRA. Use the rule of 72: if it grew 6% a year, about how many years until it doubles? How many times could it double before age 60?
