Choosing a price is one of the most important decisions a seller makes. Too high, and customers walk away. Too low, and you might not make any money.

## Start with your cost

First, find out what one item costs you to make. If a cookie costs 25 cents in ingredients, you need to charge more than 25 cents, or you lose money on every sale. Your cost is the floor for your price.

## Think about your customers

Next, ask what customers are willing to pay. A parent at a bake sale might happily pay $1 for a cookie. A classmate might only have 50 cents. Who your customers are changes what price makes sense.

## Look at other sellers

If another stand nearby sells cookies for 75 cents, many people will compare. You might match that price, go a little lower, or charge more because your cookies are bigger or tastier. Other sellers are called your **competition**.

## Price and how many you sell

A lower price usually means more sales, but less profit on each one. A higher price usually means fewer sales, but more profit on each one. The best price is often somewhere in the middle.

Here is an example with cookies that cost 25 cents each:

- At 50 cents, you sell 20 cookies and keep 25 cents each: $5 profit.
- At $1, you sell 8 cookies and keep 75 cents each: $6 profit.

In this case the higher price earned more. On another day it might not.

## Test and learn

Prices are not set forever. Try a price, watch what happens, and adjust. Businesses do this all the time.

## Try it

In Penny's Lemonade Stand game, play two days with the same weather but different prices. Compare how many cups you sold and your profit. Which price worked better?
